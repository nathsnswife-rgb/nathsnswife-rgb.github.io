Open a case and read the story behind the pin. These are the kinds of places Roadside Strange was built to help you discover.

👻 HAUNTING Gillespie Dam Bridge Reported apparitions, strange sounds and a lonely stretch of Arizona road. Read the story

The old crossing near Gillespie Dam has gathered ghost stories for years. Drivers and visitors have described shadowy figures, unexplained footsteps and the uneasy feeling that somebody is standing nearby when no one is there.

Roadside Strange treats reports like these as documented stories, not proof of the paranormal. The app pins the public location so you can explore the place, read the history and decide what you think for yourself.

🏚️ WEIRD HISTORY Vulture Mine Gold, desert ruins and stories that refused to stay buried. Read the story

Vulture Mine grew from Arizona's gold-rush history into one of the state's best-known old mining sites. Its abandoned buildings, harsh desert setting and long history have also made it a magnet for ghost stories and local legends.

It is exactly the kind of Roadside Strange stop that mixes real history with the strange stories people continue to tell about a place long after its busiest days are over.

🦶 CRYPTID The Mogollon Monster Arizona's towering forest creature, reported along the Mogollon Rim. Read the story

For decades, stories from Arizona's high country have described a large, hairy, humanlike creature moving through the forests around the Mogollon Rim. Accounts differ, but the legend has become one of the Southwest's best-known cryptid stories.

Because reports cover a broad region rather than one exact doorway or trail marker, Roadside Strange treats cases like this as regional stories and guides people toward safe public areas instead of private property.

🛸 UAP The Phoenix Lights A mass sighting that became one of Arizona's defining UAP stories. Read the story

On March 13, 1997, people across Arizona reported unusual lights moving through the night sky. Some witnesses described a formation of lights crossing the state, while later lights near Phoenix were widely associated with military flare activity.

The event remains part of modern UAP culture because of the number of witnesses, the different observations reported that night and the debate over what each group of lights represented.
